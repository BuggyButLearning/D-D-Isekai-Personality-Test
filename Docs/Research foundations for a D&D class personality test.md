# Research foundations for a D&D class personality test

A strong D&D class assessment should not behave like a meme checklist. The most defensible model is a hybrid: rank ordered strengths in the style of CliftonStrengths, continuous dimensions in the style of the Five Factor Model, activity based interest signals in the style of O*NET, and practical scenario questions in the style of situational judgment tests. Gallup’s CliftonStrengths uses paired statements and returns a profile across 34 themes grouped into four domains, the Five Factor Model measures broad dimensions on a continuum, O*NET asks about preferred work activities and maps people across six interest areas, and situational judgment tests capture how people handle realistic problems. Put together, those models imply that your D&D test should ask, “How do you usually gain power, solve problems, train, serve, and influence others?” rather than “Can you do one literal class specific thing?” citeturn16view0turn30view0turn30view1turn32search14turn16view4turn18view0turn18view1

## What existing assessments can contribute

Gallup’s CliftonStrengths is useful because it gives people both identity and nuance. The assessment takes about 30 minutes, uses paired statements, and returns a ranked profile across 34 themes instead of a single flat label. Those themes are grouped into four domains, which is why the experience feels personalized even when two people share some of the same top strengths. That ranked profile logic is extremely relevant for a D&D test, because it supports a primary class plus secondary flavor rather than forcing every person into one narrow bucket. citeturn16view0turn30view0turn30view1

The MBTI contributes narrative appeal and easy to understand result language, but it is the wrong backbone if you want a more credible mapping. Official MBTI materials describe the framework as four preference pairs that produce 16 types, and the publisher explicitly says it should never be used for recruitment or selection because it measures preferences, not skills or abilities, and does not predict behavior in that way. A systematic review and meta analysis found acceptable retest reliability for three scales but weaker reliability for Thinking Feeling, plus substantial heterogeneity across studies. For your project, the lesson is to borrow MBTI’s readability and “type story,” while avoiding hard binary logic as the main scoring engine. citeturn30view2turn16view1turn31view0

The Five Factor Model, VIA, and O*NET are better guides for the scoring design. The Five Factor Model treats personality as broad bipolar dimensions that predict important social, occupational, psychological, and health outcomes, rather than as all or nothing types. VIA gives each person a profile across 24 character strengths, again emphasizing rank order and relative emphasis. O*NET’s Interest Profiler is especially relevant because it measures six occupational interests, links them to real activities and careers, and is built to be short enough for broad public use, with an approximately 10 to 20 minute completion time. That combination strongly supports a D&D test that scores across multiple class vectors and then reports the highest pattern, not one that gates people through single novelty questions. citeturn32search14turn16view3turn30view4turn16view4

Another important lesson is that activities matter. Research on the Big Five and everyday life found that personality traits predict time spent with different people and frequency of engagement in different activities. That matters for your use case because “what people do” is often a better D&D signal than “what people say they are like.” A Bard style pattern is likely to show up in performance and audience facing behavior. A Ranger style pattern is likely to show up in fieldcraft and tracking behavior. A Wizard style pattern is likely to show up in deliberate study and documentation habits. citeturn21search6turn27view0turn28view1turn28view7turn28view11

## What psychometric practice says to do

If you want the test to feel polished and coherent, the psychometric basics still matter, even if the final product is entertainment first. Standard psychometric guidance emphasizes reliability, validity, standardized administration, and appropriate norms or comparison logic. In plain terms, that means the test needs clearly defined constructs, consistent scoring, and enough repeated evidence that one quirky answer does not decide a whole class result. citeturn18view2

The strongest questionnaire design guidance is remarkably practical. Good questions use simple familiar words, simple syntax, concrete language, exhaustive and mutually exclusive response options, and avoid leading, loaded, ambiguous, double barreled, and negatively phrased items. Good surveys also group questions by topic, begin with easier questions, move from general to specific within a topic, and push more sensitive or demanding prompts later in the experience. Those are not cosmetic details. They directly improve how interpretable and trustworthy the output feels. citeturn20view2turn20view1

Respondent burden also matters more than people think. Official UK government questionnaire guidance defines burden in terms of time, effort, frequency of contact, and stress from disturbing questions, and notes that burden lowers response quality and contributes to non response. The same guidance recommends qualitative work and cognitive testing during design. For your D&D test, that means you should pilot the draft with real users, watch where they hesitate, and simplify or split any question that asks too many things at once. citeturn20view0

Situational judgment is the missing ingredient in most fan made type quizzes. The National Academies describes situational judgment tests as measures that present hypothetical situations reflecting interpersonal, intrapersonal, or intellectual constructs, and notes their incremental validity over cognitive ability and personality measures for predicting performance in work settings. A later review summarizes best practices in writing scenarios, responses, instructions, formats, and scoring. In your design, those scenario items are how you separate nearby classes: Rogue from Bard, Fighter from Barbarian, Cleric from Paladin, Wizard from Warlock, and Druid from Ranger. citeturn18view0turn18view1

A practical design choice, based on burden guidance and the O*NET benchmark, is an experience of about 8 to 12 minutes, which usually means roughly 32 to 40 scored items plus a handful of tie breaker scenarios. O*NET shows that meaningful profiling can sit in the 10 to 20 minute range, while survey guidance warns that long instruments create fatigue and lower quality responding. citeturn16view4turn20view1turn20view0

## The real world meaning of each D&D class

**Barbarian.** Barbarian should map to dangerous physical courage, stress tolerance, and protective force, not merely “has anger issues.” Official material frames barbarians as protectors and leaders in times of war who come alive in chaotic combat and draw on rage for strength and resilience. In a modern test, the signal is effectiveness under pressure, willingness to charge into danger, and physical intensity used on behalf of self or others. citeturn28view0

**Bard.** Bard is expressive influence. Official material ties bards to music, dance, verse, inspiration, lore gathering, curiosity, and the ability to do many things well. In real life, this class should light up around performance, storytelling, public expression, persuasive charisma, and the ability to change a room’s mood. citeturn28view1

**Cleric.** Cleric is power received through devotion, service, and sacred responsibility. Official text says clerics are blessed by a deity, pantheon, or other immortal entity and often associate with temples, but it also explicitly notes that not every temple or shrine member is a cleric. So your test should look for lived calling, ritual seriousness, guiding service, and community care, not just nominal religious affiliation. citeturn28view2

**Druid.** Druid is nature as identity, not nature as hobby. Official material describes druids as revering nature above all, seeking harmony with the natural world, and sometimes pursuing a mystic union with it. Real world druid signals include ecological stewardship, plant and animal knowledge, wilderness respect, conservation behavior, and feeling spiritually grounded by natural systems. citeturn28view3turn12search0

**Fighter.** Fighter is trained competence and repeatable technique. Official text emphasizes broad familiarity across combat styles and then deep specialization in a chosen style. That translates well to people who adopt disciplined practice, tool mastery, tactical thinking, certification oriented improvement, and calm execution. Fighters should not just be “athletic”; they should feel trained. citeturn28view4

**Monk.** Monk is body and mind integrated through discipline. Official material defines monks through rigorous combat training, mental discipline, internal power, speed, and structured focus. That gives you a modern archetype built around martial arts, breath and focus practices, body control, self regulation, ascetic discipline, or other deliberate forms of embodied mastery. citeturn28view5

**Paladin.** Paladin is oath, mission, and moral commitment. Official paladin text says paladins are united by oaths, stand against corruption, uphold justice and righteousness, protect the innocent, and may draw power from commitment to justice itself as much as from a god. In a real world test, Paladin is the class of sworn causes, principled duty, mission oriented leadership, and readiness to act on conviction. citeturn29view4turn29view0

**Ranger.** Ranger is operational wilderness competence. Official material emphasizes tracking quarry like a predator, stealth in the wilds, and deadly focus used to protect the world from monsters and tyrants. In practice, this means fieldcraft, navigation, outdoors competence, tracking, watchfulness, and protector energy expressed in terrain rather than temples or institutions. citeturn28view7

**Rogue.** Rogue is cunning over brute force. Official text says rogues prefer one precise strike over a barrage and are defined by subtlety, danger avoidance, and clever supplementation. That suggests a modern archetype based on information advantage, precision, improvisation, stealth, workarounds, side door problem solving, and social dexterity rather than simple criminality. citeturn28view8

**Sorcerer.** Sorcerer is innate gift. Official text stresses power stamped into the person, often through strange events, family history, or lineage, and explicitly says sorcerers do not learn magic in the same way others do. In your test, Sorcerer should reflect natural aptitude, raw talent, prodigy energy, personal magnetism, or family legacy more than formal training. citeturn28view9

**Warlock.** Warlock is power through pact, patronage, or leverage from a greater source. Official text frames warlocks as knowledge seekers who are drawn into a binding pact with a powerful patron and who may view patrons as resources or contested allies. In real world terms, Warlock is not “works for the government.” It is someone whose power style depends on exclusive backing, sponsorship, network access, gatekeepers, or institutional leverage. citeturn28view10

**Wizard.** Wizard is learned mastery through study, documentation, and ongoing research. Official wizard text centers the spellbook, deliberate spell acquisition, arcane research, preparation, and study in other scholarly fields. This is the clearest class for formal learners, researchers, system builders, and people who turn methodical study into capability. citeturn28view11turn11search2

The most important class boundaries are these. Cleric and Paladin separate faith based service from oath based mission. Druid and Ranger separate ecological reverence from wilderness operation. Fighter and Monk separate external technique from disciplined body mind training. Wizard, Sorcerer, and Warlock separate learned power, innate power, and borrowed or patron mediated power. If those fault lines are clear in your scoring, the test will feel much smarter than the meme that inspired it. citeturn28view2turn29view4turn28view3turn28view7turn28view4turn28view5turn28view9turn28view10turn28view11

## How life activities should influence class results

Physical activity has to be treated as a family of signals, not a single one. CDC guidance distinguishes aerobic activity from muscle strengthening activity, and gives very different examples for each. A recent UCL study then found that exercise enjoyment varies by personality pattern, with extroverts tending to enjoy high intensity exercise, people high in neuroticism preferring short bursts over prolonged intensity, and conscientious people tending to show more rounded fitness and overall activity. So the test should never ask only, “Do you work out?” It should ask *how* the person trains, *why* they train, and *what kind of effort* they enjoy. citeturn24view1turn24view0

Martial arts should weight Monk heavily, and for good reason. The D&D monk is explicitly about rigorous combat training and mental discipline, and research on karate and martial arts links training with self control, emotion regulation, attention, and aspects of self regulation. That makes karate, judo, taekwondo, jiu jitsu, and similar disciplines stronger Monk signals than generic gym attendance, even though all of them count as physical activity. citeturn28view5turn25search3turn25search21

Strength sports, collision sports, and hard physical training should usually split between Fighter and Barbarian. CDC treats lifting, resistance work, and bodyweight training as muscle strengthening activity, but the class distinction comes from style and motive: methodical progression, technical mastery, and repeatable drill should push toward Fighter, while explosive force, confrontation comfort, and protector through intensity should push toward Barbarian. The same physical reality can score differently depending on whether the person’s story is “I have mastered a system” or “I become unstoppable under pressure.” citeturn24view1turn28view4turn28view0

Endurance work and high intensity conditioning are important, but they should usually act as modifiers, not as class determinants on their own. Running, cycling, swimming, and interval work say a lot about stamina and preferred effort style, but not enough about identity. They can sharpen Fighter, Ranger, Barbarian, or even Monk results depending on whether the pattern is tactical, solitary, competitive, meditative, or nature anchored. The UCL findings reinforce that exercise form and enjoyment are personality linked, which makes these activities better tie breakers than primary class gates. citeturn24view1turn24view0

Outdoor activity needs another split. Hiking, tracking, camping, navigation, hunting, search and rescue, and terrain awareness should weight Ranger because the official ranger identity is about tracking, stealth, wilderness watch, and focused protection. Conservation work, wildlife care, gardening, tree knowledge, foraging literacy, or ecological stewardship should weight Druid because the official druid identity is about reverence for nature and balance within it. “Likes the outdoors” is too vague. “Operates in the wild” and “belongs to the living world” are not the same signal. citeturn28view7turn28view3

Performance activity should map hard to Bard. The official bard identity is anchored in music, dance, verse, inspiration, lore, and audience effect. That means instruments, songwriting, dance, theater, standup, spoken word, broadcasting, voice work, teaching through charisma, and even high skill content creation should all contribute significant Bard weight. You do not want to reduce Bard to “plays guitar.” The better signal is whether the person reliably uses expression to move, persuade, entertain, or unite other people. citeturn28view1turn27view0

Education and “intelligence” also need to be broken down by kind. My Next Move describes Investigative interests as ideas, thinking, fact finding, and problem solving, while Realistic interests are practical and hands on. O*NET describes mechanical engineers in terms of research, design, evaluation, installation, operation, and maintenance. The Wizard class, meanwhile, is based on spellbooks, preparation, and ongoing research. So engineering, science, and heavily analytic technical study should usually weight Wizard first, then optionally Fighter or Ranger if the person’s work is especially field based, operational, or tool centered. citeturn27view0turn33search1turn28view11

Project management is a different pattern. O*NET describes project management specialists as coordinating schedule, timeline, procurement, staffing, and budget, and leading technical staff. O*NET also links that area heavily to Enterprising and Conventional interests. In your D&D framework, that usually looks like Paladin when the person is driven by mission and accountability, Fighter when the person is defined by execution and logistics, Bard when the person wins through coordination and communication, or Warlock when the person’s power depends mostly on institutional sponsorship and authority. In other words, a project management degree should not be read as “Wizard with a business minor.” It is usually a leadership and systems signal, not a pure scholarship signal. citeturn33search0turn34search0turn28view10turn29view4turn28view4turn28view1

This is also why direct IQ style questions are often the wrong tool. Situational judgment tests are better for your use case because they can probe practical, interpersonal, and intellectual judgment with realistic problems. A Wizard question should look like documentation, experimentation, and logic. A Rogue question should look like finding a side door under constraint. A Paladin question should look like duty under moral pressure. That is far more “class accurate” than a detached puzzle section. citeturn18view0turn18view1

Achievements should matter, but only as evidence multipliers. Belts, certifications, publications, wilderness credentials, volunteer leadership, performances, years in ministry, community organizing, entrepreneurial wins, and documented practice all help confirm patterns that the behavior questions already suggest. ## A scoring model for single class multiclass and trait variants

The strongest scoring logic is continuous, layered, and evidence based. CliftonStrengths gives ranked themes, VIA gives a full strengths profile, the Five Factor Model is continuous by design, and O*NET supports profiles across multiple interest areas rather than a single essence. For your D&D test, that means every respondent should receive a score on all 12 classes, with the final result reflecting the dominant pattern, not a brittle pass fail rule. citeturn30view0turn16view3turn32search14turn16view4turn26search0

A practical recommendation is to let each item contribute to one primary class, one secondary class, and one cross class facet. Those cross class facets are where your “unique traits” live. Good candidates are discipline, daring, empathy, scholarship, secrecy, performance, naturalism, devotion, oath, institutional leverage, and innate gift. That structure lets two people both land on Ranger while one feels like a Scout Scholar and the other feels like a Beast Tracker. It also lets two Wizards feel different, one bookish and methodical, the other inventive and theatrical. This is the real advantage of a profile model. citeturn30view0turn30view4turn27view0

For a single class result, the rule should be strict. If the top class is meaningfully ahead of the second class and clears a minimum activation floor, show one class only. That aligns with your stated design goal and avoids the lazy outcome where every interesting person becomes a multiclass. In practice, the top class should feel dominant both in *absolute* score and in *distance* from the runner up. This is a recommendation rather than a published standard, but it is consistent with the logic behind ranked strengths and continuous profiles. citeturn30view0turn16view3turn32search14

For multiclass, keep it rare and cap it at two classes. O*NET’s career tools explicitly support combining interest areas, even letting users browse three interest areas together, which shows that blended profiles are normal. But your version should only show two classes when the top two are both strong, very close together, and conceptually coherent. Cleric plus Paladin, Druid plus Ranger, Fighter plus Monk, Bard plus Rogue, Barbarian plus Fighter, and Sorcerer plus Warlock are examples that make intuitive sense because their official class identities share part of a motivational structure while still remaining distinct. citeturn26search0turn29view4turn28view2turn28view3turn28view7turn28view4turn28view5turn28view1turn28view8turn28view0turn28view9turn28view10

If one class is clearly dominant but another class leaves a small fingerprint, do not force a multiclass. Surface it as a trait badge or “alternate influence” instead. That gives you the nuance the user wants without blurring the headline result. A Paladin with Scholar traits, a Wizard with Protector traits, or a Rogue with Performer traits is usually a more satisfying and more legible answer than a forced dual class. Again, this follows the ranked profile philosophy used by CliftonStrengths and VIA better than the one label finality of a simpler personality quiz. citeturn30view0turn30view4

## A practical structure for the form and result screen

From a research standpoint, the cleanest form is one that mixes behavior history, activity signals, accomplishment evidence, and tie breaker scenarios. Because burden matters, most of the instrument should be closed ended, with only one optional open response at the very end if you want extra flavor text. Survey guidance also recommends a visible time estimate, “not applicable” where appropriate, and a short introduction that tells users what the assessment is for. citeturn20view1turn20view0

A practical blueprint looks like this:

| Section | What it measures | Suggested item count | Best response style |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Life commitments | faith, cause, nature, patronage, duty | 6 | frequency and agreement |
| Physical mode | martial practice, strength, endurance, field activity | 6 | activity checkboxes plus frequency |
| Learning mode | study, research, improvisation, documentation | 6 | behavior frequency plus scenarios |
| Social mode | performance, persuasion, mentorship, stealth | 6 | agreement plus situational choices |
| Evidence and achievements | belts, credentials, service, performances, publications | 4 | checklist plus depth follow ups |
| Tie breakers | under pressure choices and source of power | 6 | situational judgment items |

When you write the actual prompts, keep them concrete. Ask about one thing at a time, use plain language, and define time windows. For frequency questions, “in the past 12 months” is better than “in the past year,” because more explicit reference periods can produce more precise recall. Avoid vague words like “often” unless you anchor them with actual ranges or examples. citeturn20view2turn19search17turn19search4

The order of the web form matters. Start with easy, broad, non threatening questions, then move into identity relevant areas like faith, cause, or achievements, and place any optional open response at the end. That ordering follows established survey guidance and helps users warm up before they hit self reflective material. A progress indicator is worth adding because it reduces uncertainty about effort and improves completion comfort. citeturn20view1turn20view2

The result screen should be layered, not flat. The most compelling output is: a primary class, an optional multiclass subtitle if the threshold is met, three trait badges, one short explanatory paragraph grounded in the user’s dominant signals, and a minimal crest, sigil, or class banner that visualizes source of power and mode of action. That format borrows the best part of CliftonStrengths and VIA, the feeling that “this is me, but with nuance,” while staying cleaner than a wall of analytics. citeturn30view0turn30view4

Finally, the test should be framed as self reflection and entertainment, not as an ability screen. The official MBTI publisher is explicit that MBTI should never be used for recruitment or selection because it measures preferences rather than skills or abilities, and the same boundary is wise here. A D&D class test can be thoughtful, psychologically informed, and genuinely resonant without pretending to be a hiring tool or a clinical instrument. citeturn16view1
